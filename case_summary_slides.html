01 — The problem

Breakage is real revenue PayOps can't see for up to 28 days

Preply keeps 20% commission on booked hours immediately, and 100% of unbooked hours once a cycle closes. Commission is known on day 1. Breakage is only known on day 28 — and PayOps needs a number every morning, not once a month.

Day 1

Commission is known

20% of every booked hour, recognized as it happens.

Day 28

Breakage is known

100% of unbooked hours, only once the cycle closes.

Day X

This model's job

A best-guess for every open payment, refreshed every day of the wait.

02 — My approach

Two ways to guess what an open cycle will do

Method A — linear run-rate

Extrapolate this payment's own pace

Hours booked so far ÷ days elapsed × 28. Simple, and reacts fast — but with only a few lessons booked, one payment's own pace is noisy.

Method B — segment burn-curve (chosen)

Use how similar payments actually ended

What percent of a plan is typically booked by a given day, historically? Apply that curve to this payment instead of its own thin sample.

Segmented by:

  • Plan size (4h to 20h)
  • New vs. renewing student
  • Day within the cycle

Why segment, not linear, as the default

A single payment's early booking pace is too thin a sample to extrapolate 17+ days forward. A segment built from thousands of already-closed cycles of the same plan size and renewal status is a far steadier signal — confirmed by backtesting both against real outcomes (next slide). Linear stays as the fallback for segments with too little history to trust (under 20 closed cycles).

grain: 1 row per payment × snapshot_date

The key model is int_cycle_daily_progress: it re-derives "how many hours were booked as of date X" for every day of every cycle, recomputed from the full booking history rather than stored as state. Nothing can drift out of sync with reality, and it's what makes daily re-estimation possible.

03 — The data model

A trade-off I made on purpose: one mart, not three

My first draft had three marts models: a "pure" daily fact table, a denormalized dashboard version with student attributes, and a separate table just for "today's" totals. I asked myself whether that was real structure or layering for its own sake.

Kept it to one table

mart_breakage_daily carries every metric and dimension the dashboard needs — no extra join, no near-duplicate table.

Added is_current_snapshot

A flag, true on exactly one row per payment, replaces the separate "current totals" table the dashboard would otherwise need.

The real cost

Forget to filter by that flag and a plain sum() over-counts by about 29× — one row per day of the cycle, not one per payment.

I mitigated that risk instead of hiding it: a dedicated test, assert_one_current_snapshot_per_payment, fails the build if any payment ever has zero or more than one "current" row, and the column is documented in schema.yml as exactly what any new chart must filter on.

04 — Assumptions & trade-offs

Decisions I made without a clean answer available

Fallback threshold: n ≥ 20

Tested by bootstrap-resampling a large segment down to smaller sizes, 2,000 times. At n=20 the estimate can still be off by about 12.5 points — a reasonable floor, not a precision guarantee.

No seasonal adjustment in v1

Checked, not assumed: summer cycles book 71.4% of plan vs. 73.4% the rest of the year — a 2-point gap, too small to justify the added complexity.

Cycle length as a variable

28 days is a dbt var (cycle_length_days), not a hardcoded number, in every model that uses it — tested live at 30 days before shipping.

try_cast over hard casts

One bad row shouldn't fail the whole daily refresh. Trade-off: a cast failure quietly becomes NULL, caught only if the not_null tests are actually watched.

Views vs. tables by layer

Staging stays a view (thin, cost-neutral); intermediate and marts are tables — real join and aggregation work paid for once, not on every query.

29 rows kept per closed cycle

Not trimmed to the final value — needed to answer "how did the estimate evolve" and to backtest both methods. Cheap at roughly 268K rows total.

04 — Assumptions & trade-offs

What I'd warn PayOps about before they trust this

Day-28 backtest result

Is a mechanical artifact of the formula, not a genuine accuracy win — flagged, not hidden inside a clean-looking chart.

Small segments stay noisy

20h + new-student cycles (n=33) get the same estimator as an 8h renewing segment with 1,362 — same formula, much less confidence behind it.

is_current_snapshot is a convention

A dbt test catches a broken flag inside the project. A BI tool built without reading the model docs could still double-count.

No seasonal signal here isn't proof there's none

This dataset is close to flat across summer. Real Preply data may not be — worth re-checking before trusting v1 long-term.

Every open-cycle number is provisional

By construction — it will keep changing until the cycle closes. It should never carry the same confidence as a closed cycle's actual.

05 — The dashboard

What PayOps would actually open every morning

This month's total, split

What the month is expected to bring in, how much is already fact, and the commission floor underneath.

Segments compared

Plan size, new vs. renewing, and country — same scale across all three, so the comparison is honest.

How the estimate firmed up

Every morning of the month rebuilt: the total barely moves, the share of it that's known grows — filterable by cohort.

Any payment, looked up

A searchable table of all 9,695 payments: status, cycle day, breakage and rate — the case's "for any payment".

Monthly accuracy tracking

Mid-cycle estimate vs. eventual actual, month over month — watching for model drift, not checking it once.

A finding, not just a metric

Renewal rate falls from 82% to 19% as breakage rises — high breakage predicts churn more than it means free money.

05 — What's next

If I had another week

1

Validate and ship an adaptive blend of linear + segment, weighted toward linear near cycle-end — with its own bootstrap-style validation, not a hand-picked formula.

2

Turn the backtest into a standing model — MAE by segment, recomputed automatically every time a cohort closes, not a one-off analysis.

3

Add a quarantine table for staging cast failures, instead of today's silent-NULL fallback.

4

Extend the grain to answer "how much breakage lands tomorrow" directly — the model already has each open payment's real close date, as the dashboard's close-calendar view shows.

5

Hand language and learner-intent (first_subject, persona) to the Customer Insight team as a churn-risk hypothesis — real signal, but outside PayOps' revenue scope.

How I verified it
